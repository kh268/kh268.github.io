Answering hundreds of guest messages a day, automatically

The Salt Collections manages 60+ villas, serviced apartments and hotels. Staff used to answer hundreds of guest messages a day by hand. With one teammate, I built an AI system that answers the routine ones, asks guests for missing details, and hands everything else to a person.

The problem

One inbox, answered by hand, in the order messages arrived

Every guest message, from Airbnb, Agoda, Trip.com, Traveloka or Expedia, landed in one inbox. Staff answered them one by one, in the order they arrived, and that caused three problems.

Urgent messages waited. A family locked out at night sat behind someone asking about checkout time, because nothing sorted the queue.

Simple answers were slow. Every unit is different, so staff looked up the same facts again and again, or had to ask a manager.

Details were always missing. An airport pickup needs a flight number, a time, the number of guests and bags, so staff chased each piece by message.

The groundwork

Before automating anything, I built the knowledge it would answer from

There was no single place that held the answers. Each property's facts sat in its Airbnb listing, and how to answer each question lived in the heads of the customer care team. So I built that database first.

I started with the data: 160,665 past messages between guests and staff. Using Python and pandas, I cleaned them and paired them into 43,997 question-and-answer exchanges, then used AI to classify and read through them, so I knew what guests actually ask and how often. I pulled each property's basic facts out of its listings and put them all in one place, in the same format for every property. Then I worked closely with the customer care team to write down how they answer each question and to reorganise everything they knew into one structure. In the end, all of that came down to 307 approved answers and request flows.

To know what to do with a message, the system needed three things about it, so I sorted questions by three criteria. Each criterion splits them differently, and every question gets a label from all three.

1What kind of answer is it?3 categories

Some questions can't be answered until the guest tells us more. Those became Requests (41 of them): the bot keeps asking for whatever is missing, then hands the case to a person. Questions whose answer is the same at every property, with only a fact like the wifi password or the address changing, became Shared Answers (45): one template, filled with each unit's own facts. Anything genuinely different from building to building became a Property Answer (221), written in full for that property.

Why: it tells the bot whether to ask, fill in or look up, and staff fix a shared answer once instead of once per unit. For example, the wifi password is a Shared Answer, the gym's opening hours are a Property Answer, and early check-in is a Request.

2Can it go out without a person?2 categories

Stable, verified answers are sent automatically. Anything involving money, availability, exceptions or a judgment call goes to human review first.

Why: a wrong automatic answer about a refund or a free room costs far more than a slow one. For example, the pet policy goes out instantly, while an early check-in request always ends with staff, because only they know whether the room will be ready.

3Who may receive it?2 categories

The same inbox serves people still deciding whether to book and people who already have. Most answers are fine for anyone; anything with an address, wifi details, a door code or entry steps is for booked guests only.

Why: those details are a security risk in the wrong hands. For example, anyone can learn the pet policy; only a booked guest gets the wifi password.

How it works

Finding the right answer, then deciding if it may go out

Once every answer had its labels, the system's job was clear: work out what the guest needs, check what this guest is allowed to see, then reply, ask for what's missing, or pass it to staff. Technically this is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): the AI looks something up before it writes. What it looks up is the answers staff wrote and approved, and, for information questions that match none of them, the property's own facts.

Is there parking at the villa?
  1. 1. SearchFinds the three closest of 880 ways guests have asked before, like "Do you have parking?" or "Where can I leave the scooter?"
  2. 2. CheckA second AI step decides which one really means the same thing, or rejects all three. Similar wording isn't always the same question.
  3. 3. Look upPulls the parking answer written for this villa, and checks the booking before using anything private.
  4. 4. RouteSends it, asks for what's missing, or hands it to staff, depending on the answer's labels.

The rule for questions that match nothing came from a mistake. At first, when a question fell outside the database, the AI still wrote an answer, and sometimes it simply made one up. A confident wrong answer is worse than a slow one, so I took that freedom away. Now an information question that matches nothing can only be answered from the basic facts staff keep for that property. If those can't answer it safely, or the message needs a person's judgement, it goes to staff.

Example: a request that takes three messages

A question needs one answer. A pickup needs details that arrive over several messages, so the system keeps the request open and tracks what it already knows. Once the required details are in, it hands the request to staff. If the guest can't give them or needs help, it hands over earlier.

What if the guest writes again while a reply is being prepared?

Guests often add a second message a few seconds later. Before sending anything, the system checks whether a newer message has arrived. If it has, it drops the reply it was about to send and handles the newer message instead, so the guest doesn't get an answer to a question they've already changed.

The full 9-step pipelineWhat happens to a message from the moment it arrives until a reply goes out
Show detailsHide details
  1. Receive. Hostex, the tool that collects messages from every booking site, forwards each new message to our service. We save it, confirm within three seconds, and ignore duplicates.
  2. Group. Guests often send several short messages in a row, so we wait until they stop typing and treat the burst as one turn.
  3. Check access. We check whether the guest has a confirmed booking, which decides the property details their reply may use.
  4. Split. The AI separates the turn into questions, requests and small talk. Photos go straight to staff.
  5. Answer each item. Questions go through the search above, or, for information questions that match nothing, the property's facts. Requests ask only for what's still missing, and troubleshooting checks whether the guest's problem is fixed.
  6. Combine. Safe answers become one reply. If anything involves safety, payments, refunds, legal issues or access, the whole conversation goes to staff as urgent.
  7. Polish and check. The AI warms up the tone, then a final check confirms the reply answers the guest, sticks to its source and is safe. If only the polishing changed the meaning, the original wording is sent instead; if the content itself fails, the reply goes to staff. Replies are written in the guest's language.
  8. Send or hand over. Before sending, we make sure the guest hasn't written again. Anything for staff is emailed with the context and a suggested reply.
  9. Hand back. When staff reply themselves, the AI steps back. It resumes after 15 quiet minutes, and after two hours staff get an email to keep it off or turn it back on.

Privacy and safetyHow guest data and private details are protected
Show detailsHide details

Booking status is checked before restricted property details, such as an address or wifi password, are used in a reply.

Emergencies, payments, refunds, legal issues and access problems stop all automatic sending for that conversation.

Private details are hidden in the system's logs, and conversations are deleted seven days after checkout.

Links in staff emails can't be tampered with, and each on/off button works for one conversation only.

Staff accounts use two-factor sign-in, and the data is backed up with a written recovery plan.

If the AI fails or returns something malformed, the message goes to a person instead of out as a guess.

Knowledge Hub

Handing the knowledge to the people who own it

The system is only as good as what it retrieves, and the facts keep changing: a new unit, a new house rule, a broken air conditioner. My internship was going to end, and guests weren't going to stop asking. So I built the Knowledge Hub: a knowledge system the staff own, where they manage every answer the AI uses without touching code. The database I'd put together with the customer care team moved into it, and now they keep it up to date themselves.

Staff file each answer using the same three criteria and save it as a draft; a manager reviews it and publishes. The Hub refuses to save a public answer that contains an address, a password or a link, so the safety rule doesn't depend on anyone remembering it. Every edit is tied to a named account, and the whole interface works in English and Vietnamese. Behind the screens, the server enforces what each role may do, warns when someone edits an answer that changed since they opened it, and marks any search update that fails so it can be retried.

  1. 1. EditA staff member updates an answer or a property's facts and saves it as a draft.
  2. 2. CheckThe Hub blocks anything unsafe, like an address inside a public answer.
  3. 3. PublishA manager reviews the draft and publishes it.
  4. 4. UseThe search updates, and the next guest who asks gets the new answer.
How it's built and kept safeThe tools behind the Hub, and how it protects the data
Show detailsHide details
  • React and TypeScript on the front, Python (FastAPI) behind it, hosted on Vercel; 26 API endpoints and 73 automated tests.
  • Publishing an answer updates the search automatically, with a retry button if that fails.
  • The Hub and the reply service each get only the database access they need, and old versions can't be edited.
  • Every database change comes with a way to undo it and is tested on a copy first.
  • Nothing is deleted outright: properties are archived and can be restored, and every change is logged.

Results

Four in five replies, sent without anyone typing

In the first two months, about 80% of replies to guests went out automatically. That's our own operational estimate, not a controlled study. What matters more is that each of the three problems from the start now has an answer.

Urgent messages no longer wait. A message about a lockout, a payment or a safety issue goes straight to staff, marked urgent, instead of sitting behind questions about checkout time.

Simple answers go out on their own. Questions about checkout times, pool hours or hair dryers are answered from what staff already approved, so nobody looks up the same fact twice.

Requests reach staff complete. For a pickup, the system asks the guest for the flight details and the number of guests and bags, so staff get a request they can book straight away.

Handover

Built to keep running after I left

A system that only one person understands stops working when that person leaves. So the last part of my work was making sure the team could run it without me. I trained the customer service, marketing and operations teams in Vietnamese and English, wrote the guides they use, and set up staff accounts and backups.

Today the staff keep the answers up to date themselves through the Knowledge Hub, and the system is still running. None of it was a solo effort: my teammate and I designed it together and worked through every major decision side by side.

What I'd do differently

The system works, but it could be leaner. Each time, it gives the AI a fixed window of recent messages, the last six when splitting a turn, whether or not they matter to the new question. If I built it again, I'd choose the context by relevance instead: the earlier messages tied to the current question and any request still open. Then I'd measure whether that really saves tokens without losing anything important, because choosing the context has a cost of its own.
